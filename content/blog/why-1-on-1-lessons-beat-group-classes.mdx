If you've ever taken a group language class, you know the scene: eight students, ninety minutes, and if you're lucky, you spoke French or English for a grand total of four of them. Group classes survive because they're familiar — not because they work best. Here's the honest comparison.

## The speaking-time math is brutal

Language is a performance skill. You learn to speak by speaking — and speaking time is exactly what group classes can't provide.

In a typical 60-minute group class of eight students, the teacher talks roughly half the time. The remaining 30 minutes are shared eight ways: **less than 4 minutes of speaking per student**. In a 1-on-1 lesson, you speak for 25–30 minutes — every single lesson.

That's not a 20% improvement. It's **seven times** more practice per hour of your life. Over a year of weekly lessons, it's the difference between 3 hours and 25 hours of actual speaking.

## Every minute targets *your* gaps

A group curriculum has to aim at the middle of the class. Too advanced for some, too slow for others — and never about *your* specific weaknesses.

A private tutor notices that you always drop the third-person "s", that French nasal vowels are your nemesis, that you freeze on phone calls but chat happily face-to-face. The next lesson is built around exactly that. No group class can do this; it's structurally impossible.

## No hiding place (this is a good thing)

In group classes, the quiet students hide and the confident students dominate. If speaking makes you nervous — which is true of almost everyone — the group format lets you avoid the very thing you came to practice.

One-on-one, avoidance isn't an option. Your tutor asks, waits, and helps you through. Learners are often surprised how quickly the fear fades when speaking becomes a private, judgement-free routine instead of a public performance.

## Mistakes get fixed, not fossilised

Feedback delay is the silent killer of language progress. In a group, most of your errors go uncorrected — the teacher simply can't catch everything from eight people. Repeat an error for a few months and it *fossilises*: it becomes your default, and undoing it takes far longer than learning it right would have.

A private tutor corrects in real time, distinguishes the mistakes that matter from the noise, and drills the fix until it sticks.

## Scheduling that survives real life

Group classes happen when the school says so. Miss Tuesday 7pm, and the class moves on without you — momentum is exactly what a language learner can't afford to lose.
